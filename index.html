Where am I from?

I grew up in Singapore in a Sikh family where questions were welcome at the dinner table. Why do we wear a kara? Why don't goblins run our local bank, the way they do in Harry Potter? The questions got more serious over time. The habit never went away.

After the IB Diploma I spent two years in national service with the Singapore Army. An elbow injury early on took me off the usual path, so I improvised: I taught myself to write with my left hand, rebuilt my training through trial and error, and filled my evenings with whatever would teach me something. Thaler and Kahneman, online game theory courses, essay competitions, and a slightly obsessive hunt for Singapore's hidden coffee shops.

I arrived in Hanover in 2025 a couple of years older than most of my classmates, and very sure of one thing about myself: I like finding out how things actually work.

How did I end up in finance?

Through decision-making. In high school I fell for game theory. My favorite story in economics is still Axelrod's 1980 tournament, where experts submitted elaborate, ruthless strategies for a repeated prisoner's dilemma, and the winner was two lines of code called tit-for-tat. Then behavioral economics explained why the tidy models kept missing real people.

I first watched both ideas work in real life while co-leading Project Ancora, a student fundraiser for stray animals in Singapore. Going door to door, we noticed that our best days always started with generous neighbors. Their amounts on the sign-up sheet anchored everyone who came after. Later we added a prize for the top fundraiser and posted daily updates, and volunteers stopped comparing their $25 to zero and started comparing it to the leader's $180. Nobody got more generous. The setup changed, and so did the behavior.

Markets are the same problem at a larger scale. Every price is someone's decision about what someone else will do, with real money attached.

That is what pulls me toward investing. It rewards the person who asks why one more time than everyone else, and it keeps score.

What did this summer teach me?

This summer I interned at a seed-stage insurance startup in Singapore. I was hired to build automation, tools that turn engineering changes into documentation so the team spends less time writing it by hand. But the most interesting file in the company was the financial model, and I wanted to understand it. I asked my supervisor whether I could study it, then offered the executive chairman a second pair of eyes.

Most of the model was sound. One line was not. A piece of subordinated debt was being counted toward the company's capital with no interest cost attached, which made the business look sturdier than it was. It was one line in a very large spreadsheet, and for a moment I wondered whether the intern should be the one to raise it with the most senior person in the room. Then I thought about who would read that model next: people deciding how much money to put in. I raised it. He thanked me, we worked through the fixes together, and my summer quietly turned from automation into modeling and strategy.

The bigger lesson was about what an insurer really is. Underneath the policies, a life insurer is an investor holding a very long promise. It collects premiums today, invests them for decades, and has to earn more than it promised its policyholders. Once I saw that, a lot of financial news started to make sense, especially why private equity and private credit firms have spent the last few years buying, backing and lending alongside insurers. That overlap between insurance balance sheets and private markets is the thing I most want to understand next.

What am I learning right now?

My current rule: if I can't rebuild it by hand, I don't understand it yet. Here is what's on my desk this fall. Open any of them for the why.

Accounting and valuation, one company at a timeChipotle, from burritos to a DCF

I put together a 45-day self-study plan that runs every concept through a single real business, Chipotle. Why does profit disagree with cash? Why does a DCF swing so much when you nudge the discount rate by half a percent? It's easier to remember an answer when it has a burrito attached. I use AI to design the plan and quiz me. The Excel work I do by hand, because that's the part that sticks.

Why Copart's missing cars might not be missingA research note I'm writing on insurance cycles

Copart auctions the cars insurers write off after accidents. Recently its insurance volumes fell even though there are more cars on the road. My working theory is that after two years of steep premium increases, a lot of drivers dropped collision coverage or raised their deductibles. The crashed cars still exist. They just never reach an insurer, so they never reach Copart.

If premiums keep easing, some of that coverage should come back, with a lag. The question I keep returning to is whether people who stretched their deductibles ever go back, or whether that habit sticks. That one is as much behavioral economics as it is finance, which is probably why I like it.

Secondaries, the market I didn't know existedWhere investors trade stakes in funds with each other

A conversation this September with an analyst at a secondaries fund introduced me to a corner of private equity where investors buy existing fund stakes from other investors who want their money back early. It solved a puzzle I'd had about how anyone exits a ten-year commitment. I'm reading my way in, starting with how those stakes get priced when nobody agrees on what the underlying companies are worth today.

Grab, rebuilt from zeroRevisiting a stock pitch without templates

In the spring I pitched Grab in a stock pitch competition. My argument was that the market was still anchored on the company's early losses rather than the business it had become. This fall I've committed to rebuilding that valuation from a blank workbook, to check whether my conclusion survives when I can't lean on anyone else's model.

Why Recalc?

Recalc says it teaches the why behind the technical skills. For someone who grew up as Mr. Why, that sentence is most of the reason I'm applying.

I'm a sophomore, right on the program's timeline, and I've spent this fall building my accounting and valuation foundations on my own. Self-study has taken me a long way. What it can't give me is people who have sat on the investing side of the table telling me which of my instincts hold up and which don't. I want to learn to take a business apart the way investors do, from people who have done the work, alongside a cohort that pushes me to be sharper.

What I'd bring back: a lens on insurance and private credit that few students my age have spent time with, a habit of building small AI tools that make a group faster (which I'd happily share with the cohort), and a willingness to be the one in the Slack who asks the question everyone else was quietly wondering about.

If anything here raised a question, I'd love to hear it. Questions are kind of my thing.
